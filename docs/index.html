Can a year of production always be completed?

The 2018 Supply and Use Tables of the Philippines give, for each industry, what it produced and what it used in the year. They do not give the order in which production happened. This study simulates the year one batch at a time: an industry can run a batch only when all of its inputs are in stock. It asks two questions.

Q1How much opening stock is needed so that every simulated year is completed, and what share of years is completed with less?
Q2When the stock is limited, do the completed years share an order of industries?

1 · The data

The table records totals, not the order

What does the 2018 table say about the year?

The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) publishes the 2018 tables with 240 products and 240 industries. This study collapses them to 80 products and 80 industries with PSA's input–output classification: the rows of products in the same group are added, and the columns of industries in the same group are added. In the year plan of the study, after rounding to whole units, the totals are:

For every product, supply equals use for the year as a whole. But the table does not say whether the inputs of a batch were in stock at the moment the batch ran. That depends on the order of production, which the table does not record.

2 · Two views

Two timeless views of the same table

Equilibrium

Supply and demand meet at a vector of prices, and all the equations of the economy hold at the same time (Arrow and Debreu, 1954). The input–output model of Leontief (1936) also solves for all outputs at once. Nothing happens before or after anything else: the equations are solved as if instantly.

Asks: are the flows of the year consistent with one another?

Concurrent processes (this study)

Production is many concurrent processes, that is, processes whose order is not fixed in advance. Each one can run whenever its inputs are in stock, and its outputs become inputs for others. Bonanno (1993, 1995) showed how to read an input and an output matrix as a Petri net.

Asks: can the year be completed step by step from a given stock, and in which order?

Both views are timeless when compared with dynamic models of the economy, in which production takes time and stocks pass from one period to the next (Kydland and Prescott, 1982). Neither view has a calendar. In this study each industry works in 52 equal batches. The number 52 is used because it is easy to read as weeks, but a batch is not a calendar week.

The same stock, two different years

Three industries, three products

Farming, Factory and Power plant each run 3 batches in the year. A batch can run only if the stock holds all of its inputs. It then takes its inputs from the stock and adds its output.

Order A completes the year

Start with the stock (crops, goods, power) = (4, 4, 5): exactly the inputs of one batch of each industry. Run Power plant, Farming, Factory, and repeat this three times.

After each round the stock is (6, 6, 6), then (8, 8, 7), then (10, 10, 8). All 9 batches run. The final stock is the opening stock plus (6, 6, 3), which is what the year delivers to final users.

Order B stops the year

Start again from (4, 4, 5) and run the Factory twice. The stock becomes (2, 9, 3) and then (0, 14, 1).

No crops are left, and every batch needs crops, so nothing can run. Seven batches are left, and the year stops. Same stock, same recipes: one order completes, the other stops.

The stock decides how many orders complete

The 9 batches can be arranged in 9!/(3!·3!·3!) = 1,680 orders. A random year chooses, at each step, one of the batches that can run, with equal chances.

More stock does not always help

From (10, 10, 9) no random year stops. From (10, 10, 10), 10.5% of random years stop. The net shows one of them: Farming three times and Factory twice use all the power, and then neither the last Factory batch nor the Power plant can run.

With 9 units of power, the second Factory batch cannot run, so the Power plant runs instead and the year continues. In the same way, 63% of random years complete from (6, 6, 7) but 57% from (6, 6, 8). Each stock level must be studied on its own.

An order among the completed years

At (6, 6, 8), 35% of the completed random years had a step at which some industry had to wait for an input. In the completed years, the Power plant's n-th batch comes before the Factory's n-th batch in 57%, 53% and 53% of cases (n = 1, 2, 3). Without an order, each share would be 50%.

At (10, 10, 13) no batch ever waits and every year completes, and every such share is exactly 50%: there is no order.

At (10, 10, 10) no completed year waits, but some years stop, and the share for n = 1 is 56%: the years in which the Factory ran early are the ones that stopped.

4 · The 2018 net

The 2018 table as a net

How does the 2018 table become a net like the small example?

Each of the 80 products is a place, and each of the 80 industries is a transition. One batch of an industry uses 1/52 of its yearly inputs and makes 1/52 of its yearly output. Each imported product arrives in 52 equal deliveries.

Whole units. Each product is counted in whole units. The size of one unit is chosen for each product, from 0.1 pesos to 100,000 pesos, and the inputs and outputs of each batch are rounded half up.

Opening stock in weeks of use. An opening stock of w weeks holds, for every product, w/52 of the year's input use of that product, rounded up to a whole unit. So 26 weeks is half of a year's input use.

Random rule. At each step, one of the batches or deliveries that can run is chosen, with equal chances. A year stops if nothing can run while batches are left. A completed year ends with the opening stock plus what final users receive.

6 · Results for Q1

How much opening stock is needed?

With how many weeks of stock is every simulated year completed, and what share is completed with less?

Show the numbers as a table

Each point is 1,000 simulated years

For 9 levels of opening stock, the study simulated 1,000 years each. The top panel shows the share of years completed, with its 95% interval (Wilson, 1927). The bottom panel shows the share of all steps at which some industry with batches left had to wait.

Below 9 weeks, many years stop

With 2 weeks of stock, 9.5% of years complete, and some industry waits at 86% of steps. With 4 weeks, 45.8% complete.

More stock, fewer completed years

With 9 weeks, 91.9% of years complete; with 13 weeks, only 85.0%. The difference is larger than chance (Fisher's exact test, p ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁶). As in the small example, each level of stock must be studied on its own.

The year at 13 weeks in Section 5 shows one way this happens.

Stops become rare, but they do not disappear

No year stopped with 17 or with 22 weeks, but 5 of 1,000 stopped with 26 weeks. With 1,000 years, stops rarer than about 3 in 1,000 can go unseen, so "no stop seen" at 17 and 22 weeks does not mean "no stop possible".

The search

The study then searched between 26 weeks, the largest stock tried with a stop, and 35 weeks. Each try simulated 1,000 years at the middle of the interval. No try had a stop, so each try became the new upper end: 30.5, 28.25, 27.13, 26.56, 26.28 and 26.14 weeks.

The result is w*: the smallest stock tried above the largest stock tried at which some year stopped. Here w* = 26.14 weeks.

The check

Because the search chose w*, its own runs are not a fair test. The study simulated 1,000 new years at w*, and none stopped. So, with 95% confidence, fewer than 3 in 1,000 years stop at 26.14 weeks (the rule of three; Hanley and Lippman-Hand, 1983).

The answer to Q1 for now: 26.14 weeks, or 9.19 trillion pesos, about half of a year's input use. This does not show that every larger stock completes every year.

7 · Q2: the order of industries

How the order is tested, and what was found

In the years that complete, do some industries tend to run earlier than others?

The small example gives a case where they do. At (6, 6, 8), the Power plant's n-th batch comes before the Factory's n-th batch in more than half of the completed years. At (10, 10, 13), every share is 50%.

The study asks the same of the 2018 net. The figure below goes through each step of the test (Section 7 of the paper) on years that your browser simulates now: 100 completed years with 9 weeks of stock, where industries often wait, and 100 reference years with 52 weeks of stock, where nothing waits. These are not the study's years; the study's results come at the end of this section.

The test, step by step

When in the year does a batch run?

Take one simulated year with 9 weeks of stock, and one industry, Manufacture of food products. Its 52 batches run at different steps of the year; each tick in the figure is one batch.

The moment of a batch is its step divided by the 7,644 firings of the year: 0 is the start of the year, 1 is the end. In a reference year (52 weeks of stock), nothing waits, and the batches spread evenly over the year.

Choose another industry above the figure, or another year below it.

One number per year

For every year, the average moment of the industry's 52 batches is one number. The top row has one dot per simulated year at 9 weeks. The bottom row has one dot per reference year; each of its dots averages all 80 industries, because in the reference years all industries behave alike.

The difference between the two rows, in weeks, is the industry's lead: positive means later than the reference, negative means earlier.

Is the difference larger than chance?

Another set of 100 years would give a slightly different difference. The standard error (se) measures how much. It adds the uncertainty of the two averages, each from its own row: se = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂), where s is the spread of a row and n its number of years.

The t-score counts how many standard errors the difference lies from zero: t = d / se. Welch's t-test keeps the two spreads separate, because here they are very different: the bottom row is much narrower than the top row.

Move the slider under the figure: with fewer years, the standard error grows, the 95% interval widens, and t falls.

What does chance alone give?

How large can t be when there is no order? The page cuts the reference years into two random halves 2,000 times, and each time compares a random industry in one half with the reference in the other half. No industry has an order there, so these t-scores show chance alone.

They pile up between about −2 and 2, and follow Student's t-distribution (the black curve), which the test uses. The p-value is the area of that curve beyond the observed t, on both sides: the chance of a t-score at least this far from 0 when there is no order.

80 tests at once

The test runs for each of the 80 industries. Even with no order at all, about 4 of 80 tests would give p < 0.05 by chance.

The false-discovery rate controls this (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995): sort the 80 p-values, compare the k-th smallest with 0.05 × k/80 (the black line), and keep as findings all the tests up to the last one below the line. Then, on average, at most 5% of the findings are false.

The button "check" runs the same tests on the reference years against themselves, as the paper does. It should find nothing.

Is the ranking the same early and late in the year?

An order of industries should look the same in the first and in the second half of the batches. The page ranks the 80 industries by their early batches (1 to 26) in the even-numbered years, and by their late batches (27 to 52) in the odd-numbered years. Using different years keeps chance from making the two rankings agree.

Kendall's τ compares the two rankings pair by pair: +1 if every pair of industries has the same order in both rankings, 0 if pairs agree as often as they disagree.

Who runs before whom?

For two industries a and b: in what share of the 52 batches does a's n-th batch run before b's n-th batch? Without an order, about one half. The figure shows this share for every year.

A t-test of the yearly shares against 0.5, for all 3,160 pairs of industries, with the false-discovery rate of 0.05, decides which pairs get a link a → b. If the links contain no cycle, they form a DAG (directed acyclic graph): an order in which some pairs stay unordered.

What the study found

The study ran the same steps on 1,000 years at w* = 26.14 weeks, where only 0.6% of the years had any waiting. It found no industry earlier or later than the reference, τ = 0.017, and no link.

The table compares this with the years simulated on this page. At w*, they also show no order. At 9 weeks, where industries wait, the same steps can find a difference; the table shows what they find in these 100 years. The study has not yet run this case: it is the next step of the paper.

The same analysis on the reference years alone (even years against odd years) finds 0 industries and 0 links, as it should. At 26.14 weeks industries almost never wait, so there is no reason for an order, and none is found. At this stock, the answer to Q2 is no.

Next step. Repeat the analysis at a stock where industries wait, such as 9 weeks: there, 16.6% of steps had a waiting industry, and 8.1% of years stopped.

At such a stock an order among the completed years can have two sources: waiting, and the selection of the years that complete, because the stopped years are not in the analysis (as at (10, 10, 10) in the small example).

8 · Limits

What is preliminary

  • These results come from one run of the study code, with 1,000 simulated years per stock and 9 levels of stock between 2 and 52 weeks.
  • Only one rule chooses the order: equal chances among the batches and deliveries that can run. Other rules can give other answers.
  • The table is collapsed from 240 to 80 sectors, the year is divided into 52 batches, and every batch is rounded to whole units. Each of these choices can change where years stop.
  • Imports arrive as 52 equal deliveries at random steps, and products for final users stay in stock until the end of the year.
  • Q2 has been analyzed only at w* = 26.14 weeks, where there is almost no waiting.
  • The browser simulation on this page uses the same plan and rule as the study, but other random numbers: its counts differ from the study's by chance.
